# Most guardrail benchmarks let publishers hide false blocks

Nobody else benchmarks guardrails the way raxIT does, and the differences mostly favour raxIT. Red-team tools such as promptfoo, garak, PyRIT and DeepTeam measure how often attacks get through, grade the outcome with an LLM judge, and mostly ignore benign traffic. Prompt-injection and content-safety benchmarks score rows against labels, but almost all of them let each system pick its own operating point: "optimally configured" vendors, "best F1", "optimal F1", or the best strict-or-loose mode per dataset. Vendor head-to-heads that include Bedrock, Azure or Model Armor are written by competitors who rank themselves first and withhold their data. Across all five strands of research, we found no guardrail board that publishes paired confidence intervals, multiple-comparison control or an independent cost per check. raxIT's fixed 0.5 threshold, balanced accuracy, paired bootstrap with Holm tiers and cost per 1,000 checks are already stricter than the field. The weak points are elsewhere. A static set of public rows says little about adaptive robustness. Public rows are heavily exposed to training data. Gold labels on safety data carry real noise. And the edition 3 pilot's main problem, attacks too obvious to separate strong systems, is exactly what the adaptive-attack literature predicts. The lessons for raxIT are mostly about the hard set: write attacks that do not look malicious, score benign twins as their own axis, call the held-back slice semi-private, and report the views that the field uses to expose over-blocking.

## Four kinds of evaluation, each measuring a different job

The tools people call "guardrail benchmarks" answer four different questions, and only one of them is raxIT's question.

Red-team scanners measure attack success against a whole application. promptfoo is the most careful about guardrails specifically. Its guide says a useful eval "measures both sides of the policy" and names four metrics: attack block rate, false-positive rate, an **indeterminate rate** for timeouts, skipped filters and errors, and latency and cost ([promptfoo](https://www.promptfoo.dev/docs/guides/testing-guardrails/)). It warns that a missing guardrail signal currently behaves like `flagged: false`, so "a green test with no `guardrails` object proves nothing about enforcement", and that Bedrock detect-only findings are not interventions ([promptfoo](https://www.promptfoo.dev/docs/configuration/expected-outputs/guardrails/)). It also separates direct testing of the guardrail service, which suits vendor comparison and threshold tuning, from integrated testing of the full app ([promptfoo](https://www.promptfoo.dev/docs/guides/testing-guardrails/)). promptfoo has published no head-to-head numbers for guardrail products, and OpenAI announced in March 2026 that it would acquire the company ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/blog/openai-to-acquire-promptfoo)). That matters for raxIT, which scores an OpenAI Decisions API.

garak scores only attack failures. It reports an absolute pass rate plus a Z-score against a calibration "bag" of other models, bins both into grades 1 to 5, and aggregates with the **minimum** "to avoid obscuring important failures" ([garak docs](https://reference.garak.ai/en/latest/reporting.html)). PyRIT, Giskard and DeepTeam all grade with LLM judges. PyRIT is the only one that publishes how good its judges are: about **89.6% accuracy** for its best refusal scorer against human labels ([PyRIT](https://microsoft.github.io/PyRIT/latest/code/scoring/scorer-metrics)). An independent Fujitsu study of garak, Giskard, PyRIT and CyberSecEval found evaluator misclassification "can reach 37%" ([Brokman et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16527v2)). promptfoo's own CTO argues that attack success rate is not portable. The same attack can shift by **50 points or more** depending on attempt budget, prompt set and judge, and two judges that are both 80% accurate can report 46% and 60% on the same true 50% rate ([promptfoo blog](https://www.promptfoo.dev/blog/asr-not-portable-metric/)).

The second family, detector and guard-model benchmarks, scores single texts against labels, as raxIT does. The third family, agent environments such as AgentDojo, InjecAgent and BIPIA, has no row labels at all. A detector there is scored only by the agent's attack success and utility ([AgentDojo](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.13352v3)). The fourth is vendor comparisons, covered below. raxIT sits in the second family and should say so: it measures direct classification, not end-to-end blocking and not robustness to an adaptive attacker. Its use of dataset labels instead of an LLM judge removes the 10 to 37% judge error that red-team numbers carry, which is worth stating on the methodology page.

## Publishers usually choose the threshold after seeing the data

The threshold question splits the field, and most published numbers come from an operating point that was picked with the answers in view. Lakera's PINT leaderboard lists Lakera Guard at 95.22%, Bedrock at 89.24% and Model Armor at 70.07%, with every system "optimally configured" and the configs kept in a separate file ([PINT](https://github.com/lakeraai/pint-benchmark)). Fiddler's report gives "best F1", and Bedrock and Azure return only low, medium or high, so the report never says how those were mapped to a block ([Fiddler](https://www.fiddler.ai/guardrails-benchmarks/access)). ShieldGemma reports "optimal F1" without saying how the threshold was chosen ([ShieldGemma](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21772)). Qwen3Guard averages its tables using "the optimal mode per benchmark", treating controversial content as unsafe on one dataset and safe on another ([Qwen3Guard](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.14276)).

A few sources do fix the threshold. Granite Guardian computes precision, recall and F1 at **0.5** and adds recall at fixed false-positive rates of 0.1, 0.01 and 0.001 ([Granite Guardian](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.07724)). Fomin's "When Benchmarks Lie" uses fixed thresholds of 0.5 and 0.9 and notes that per-fold optimal thresholds vary widely ([Fomin](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.14161)). Detector papers prefer a low-FPR operating point, and the reason is instructive. Prompt Guard 1 and 2 have nearly the same AUC, .987 and .998, yet recall at 1% FPR jumps from **21.2% to 97.5%** ([Prompt Guard 2 card](https://huggingface.co/meta-llama/Llama-Prompt-Guard-2-86M)). AUC hides the deployable gap; a fixed low-FPR point exposes it.

This makes raxIT's fixed 0.5 a real point of difference rather than a limitation. It answers "what happens if you deploy with defaults", which is the question a buyer has. It does mix discrimination with calibration, so a system with good ranking but a poorly placed default looks worse than it is. The field's fix is a second view: AUROC or recall at a fixed FPR for systems that expose a score, plus an oracle threshold labelled as an upper bound. Systems that return only a flag, such as Bedrock, get a single ROC point, and the board should say so.

## Hard negatives and labelling policy decide the rankings

Every benchmark that builds benign rows which look like attacks finds that detectors fall apart on them. That is the same pattern raxIT sees on direct prompt attacks at 74.4 and content requests at 86.8. About 94% of PINT's rows are benign, and **20.9% are deliberate hard negatives** ([PINT](https://github.com/lakeraai/pint-benchmark)). NotInject builds 339 benign sentences around 113 mined trigger words. On it, Prompt Guard 1 scores **0.88%** over-defense accuracy, deepset 5.31% and Lakera Guard 87.61% ([PIGuard paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.22770v3)). Fomin measured deepset's benign false-positive rate at **82.4%** ([Fomin](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.14161)). In AgentDojo, adding the ProtectAI detector cut targeted attack success from 57.69% to 7.95% but dropped benign utility from 69.0% to 41.49%, and the authors say the detector "has too many false positives" ([AgentDojo](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.13352v3)).

On content safety, much of the over-blocking is a policy disagreement, not a capability gap. "Policy Is Not Capability" trained ten arms of the same architecture under different labelling policies. Catch rate on HarmBench-copyright spread **23 times** between arms, and the copyright and OR-Bench-hard scores were anti-correlated at Spearman −0.87. Counting "boundary safe" rows as unsafe moved over-refusal from **5.7% to 96.0%** with no model change. Granite Guardian scored 0 and Qwen3Guard-4B scored 99 on the same 75 copyright prompts ([Jebraeeli et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.25060)). GuardBench showed that swapping only the policy prompt lifted Mistral-7B to state of the art on several datasets ([GuardBench](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.1022.pdf)). The benign labels themselves are softer than they look. OR-Bench keeps a prompt as "safe" when a majority of GPT-4-turbo, Llama-3-70b and Gemini-1.5-pro say it is safe ([OR-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.20947)), so a guard that blocks an OR-Bench-hard row is partly disagreeing with three LLMs. XSTest is the cleaner human anchor, with Fleiss κ of 0.89 to 0.97, but has only 250 safe prompts ([XSTest](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.01263)).

For raxIT, this changes how to read the content and prompt-attack headroom. Some of the 86.8 reflects systems trained on a broader block policy, not systems that are worse at reading text. Folding benign false blocks into one balanced-accuracy number hides that. The NotInject table, Llama Guard's FPR column and Artificial Analysis's recall-versus-over-refusal framing all show it as a separate axis ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/de/articles/guardrail-safety-benchmark)).

## Public rows leak into training, and gold labels are noisy

Training overlap is the field's least disclosed problem, and it hits raxIT's public-row design directly. One lab measured exact-match overlap in its own training pools of **88% for JailbreakBench-benign and 82% for OR-Bench-hard**, and after decontamination only two over-refusal benchmarks stayed usable ([Jebraeeli et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.25060)). GuardReasoner and PolyGuard train on the train splits of the benchmarks they report, and WildGuard trains on Aegis train while testing on Aegis test ([GuardReasoner](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.18492); [WildGuard](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.18495)). Fomin's leave-one-dataset-out study found same-source splits inflate AUC by **8 to 16.5 points**, and 28% of top features act as dataset shortcuts ([Fomin](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.14161)). ProtectAI's own card shows the drop from 99.93% in-distribution to 95.25% on unseen datasets ([ProtectAI](https://huggingface.co/protectai/deberta-v3-base-prompt-injection-v2)). GuardBench's simple fix is an asterisk on every cell where a model trained on that dataset's train split ([GuardBench](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.1022.pdf)).

Held-back data helps but does not stay private when it goes to hosted APIs. The BIG-bench canary reappeared in GPT-4-base output, and the GPT-4 report excluded BIG-bench for contamination ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/kSmHMoaLKGcGgyWzs/big-bench-canary-contamination-in-gpt-4)). ARC Prize calls its API-tested holdout "semi-private" because it is "exposed to commercial APIs and thus carry some risk of leakage", and it flags overfitting when the semi-private and public scores differ by more than 10 points ([ARC-AGI-2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.11831); [ARC Prize 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.04604)). Scale SEAL restricts entries from developers who may have seen prompts via API logging ([Scale](https://scale.com/leaderboard/methodology)). raxIT's 60% held-back hard set will reach every hosted system on every run, which makes it semi-private by ARC's definition.

Label noise sets a ceiling on what point differences mean. WildGuardTest reports Fleiss κ of 0.55 for prompt harm and 0.50 for response harm, and Aegis reports about 74% raw agreement ([WildGuard](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.18495); [Aegis 2.0](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.09004)). Northcutt and colleagues found at least 3.3% label errors on average across ten major test sets, enough to flip model rankings ([Northcutt et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.14749)). promptfoo's audit of ten "Political Activities" prompts found one clear violation, seven borderline and two clearly fine, in a category a paper reported at over 80% attack success ([promptfoo blog](https://www.promptfoo.dev/blog/asr-not-portable-metric/)). Krippendorff's convention is α of at least 0.80 for firm conclusions and 0.667 as the floor for tentative ones ([Krippendorff's alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krippendorff%27s_alpha)). No guardrail benchmark we found publishes an α at all.

## Static rows miss adaptive attacks, and the pilot shows why

Static benchmarks overstate detector robustness by a wide margin. "The Attacker Moves Second" used gradient search, reinforcement learning, evolutionary search and a $20k human red-team event against twelve defences. Adaptive attacks beat ProtectAI, Prompt Guard and Model Armor **over 90% of the time**, and PIGuard held best at 71%. The authors observed that "many successful triggers weren't obviously malicious", so detectors could not flag them without more false positives, and stacking detectors did not help ([Nasr et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.09023v1)). Character injection such as homoglyphs and zero-width characters reached up to 100% evasion against six detectors including Azure Prompt Shield ([Hackett et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11168)). Spacing out characters made Prompt Guard 86M miss almost every malicious prompt ([Cisco](https://blogs.cisco.com/security/bypassing-metas-llama-classifier-a-simple-jailbreak)). In LLMail-Inject, teams needed about **506 attempts** for a first success against an LLM judge and about 89 against a text classifier, and no Phase 2 submission beat all stacked defences ([LLMail-Inject](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.09956v2)).

The edition 3 pilot result fits this evidence. The top three systems agreed on 91% of rows and caught every attack, losing points only on benign twins. Hand-written attacks that read as attacks are easy for any modern decision model. Nasr's finding says the attacks that work look benign. The hard set therefore needs attack twins that do not announce themselves, and some of those need to come from an adaptive process: search against open-weight systems on raxIT's own VM, then transfer to hosted ones. Attempts-to-first-success, as LLMail reports, is a usable robustness number that a static board cannot produce. The pilot also confirms that benign twins carry the signal, which matches PINT, NotInject and AgentDojo. That is useful, but a board whose only spread comes from over-blocking measures policy breadth as much as detection skill.

## The field reports almost no uncertainty and almost no cost

raxIT's statistics are well ahead of comparable boards. GuardBench, the guard-model papers, PINT and Artificial Analysis report no confidence intervals or significance tests ([GuardBench](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.1022.pdf); [Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/guardrail-safety-benchmark)). Fomin's DeLong intervals on AUC are the only comparable practice in the detector literature ([Fomin](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.14161)). The general eval literature points the same way raxIT already went, with one or two extra steps. Miller recommends clustering standard errors on the unit of randomisation, analysing paired differences and running a power analysis before building a set ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/statistical-approach-to-model-evals)). Bowyer and colleagues show that normal-approximation intervals "usually dramatically underestimat[e] uncertainty" below a few hundred items and collapse at 0% or 100% ([Bowyer et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.01747)). That matters for hard-set categories of about 150 pairs and for systems at 100% on saturated jobs. "The Leaderboard Lottery" finds the reported winner is the true best only 0 to 58% of the time on a calibrated Open LLM Leaderboard simulation, and proposes a paired-bootstrap set of plausible winners with family-wise error control ([Jain, ICML 2026](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/80012)). LMArena publishes a rank spread for the same reason ([LMArena](https://news.lmarena.ai/ranking-method)).

Cost is the thinnest dimension. Fiddler's vendor report is the only source with per-call cost for Bedrock and Azure, quoting $0.15 and $0.38 per 1,000 text units against its own $0.014 to $0.021, at list prices and with no named datasets ([Fiddler](https://www.fiddler.ai/guardrails-benchmarks/access)). Artificial Analysis gives the clearest latency protocol: sequential requests, 20 warm-up calls discarded, mean and p95, named hardware, and a flag that hosted-API latency is not comparable to self-hosted latency. It gives no cost ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/guardrail-safety-benchmark)). PINT, the dated table that the market already quotes, shows a test date on every row and nothing else ([PINT](https://github.com/lakeraai/pint-benchmark)).

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing of raxIT's open problems more than its method. The fixed threshold, the label-based scoring and the Holm tiers answer the main critiques of the field: oracle operating points, judge error and single-number rankings. What raxIT has not yet answered is the critique that applies to every static, public-row board. Its rows may be in training data, its labels carry noise that nobody has measured, and its attacks do not adapt. The edition 3 pilot ran into the third problem in miniature. The hard set will separate strong systems only if its attacks are written, or found, the way successful real attacks look, which is benign.

A second shift is about what the headline means. Where the board is open, it is open because of false blocks on benign rows, and the policy literature shows those depend heavily on which policy a system was trained to. Putting over-blocking on its own axis turns that from a hidden weakness of the single number into the board's most useful finding for buyers.

## Recommendations, in priority order

1. **Change how edition 3 attacks are written.** Require that each attack twin pass a "does this look malicious" screen by a human or a weak open model before acceptance, and generate part of the set by adaptive search against open-weight systems on the GPU VM, then freeze it. Evidence: the pilot's top three caught every attack, and Nasr et al. found successful adaptive triggers "weren't obviously malicious" ([Nasr et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.09023v1)).
2. **Call the held-back slice semi-private and keep a small private slice.** Hosted APIs see every held-back row. Label the 60% "semi-private", keep a smaller slice that runs only on open-weight systems on raxIT hardware, document each provider's retention settings, and flag any system whose public and held-back scores differ by more than a pre-set gap. Rely on rotation, not the canary. Evidence: ARC's semi-private tier and 10-point check, and the BIG-bench canary leak ([ARC Prize 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.04604); [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/kSmHMoaLKGcGgyWzs/big-bench-canary-contamination-in-gpt-4)).
3. **Report over-blocking as its own axis.** Next to balanced accuracy, show attack recall and benign false-block rate per job, and on the hard set report pair accuracy, the share of pairs where both twins are blocked, and the share where both are allowed. Evidence: NotInject's three-column table and the 5.7% to 96.0% over-refusal swing from mapping alone ([PIGuard](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.22770v3); [Jebraeeli et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.25060)).
4. **Keep the fixed 0.5 headline and add a labelled secondary view.** For systems that expose a score, publish AUROC and recall at 1% false-block rate, plus an oracle threshold marked as an upper bound. Plot every system's operating point on one ROC plane and mark flag-only systems as single points. Evidence: Prompt Guard's AUC of .987 vs .998 hiding a 21% vs 97.5% gap, and Granite Guardian's fixed-FPR appendix ([Prompt Guard 2](https://huggingface.co/meta-llama/Llama-Prompt-Guard-2-86M); [Granite Guardian](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.07724)).
5. **Resample pairs, not rows, and fix small-cell intervals.** Cluster the edition 3 bootstrap on the twin pair. Use Wilson or Clopper-Pearson intervals for category cells and for systems at or near 100%. Run a power analysis on the smallest category and publish the minimum detectable difference before release. Evidence: Miller's clustering and power advice and Bowyer et al. on small-n intervals ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/statistical-approach-to-model-evals); [Bowyer et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.01747)).
6. **Add a set of plausible winners to the Holm tiers.** Publish a paired-bootstrap "could be best" set or a rank spread, which answers the reader's question more directly than pairwise tiers. Evidence: the Leaderboard Lottery and LMArena ([Jain](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/80012); [LMArena](https://news.lmarena.ai/ranking-method)).
7. **Disclose training overlap.** Add a per-source table with an asterisk wherever a system's maker says it trained on that source, and run a dataset-identity classifier as a shortcut check. Evidence: GuardBench's asterisk, the 82 to 88% overlap on OR-Bench-hard and JBB-benign, and Fomin's 8 to 16.5 point inflation ([GuardBench](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.1022.pdf); [Jebraeeli et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.25060); [Fomin](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.14161)).
8. **Audit labels on the public rows and pre-register α on the hard set.** Use the 20 systems' outputs to flag rows most systems "get wrong" with confidence, have humans review a sample, and publish the borderline share. For edition 3, pre-register α of at least 0.80 per category, use a third adjudicator, and publish contested pairs as a separate unscored slice. Evidence: WildGuardTest κ of 0.50 to 0.55, Northcutt's 3.3% error rate, and promptfoo's 7-of-10 borderline audit ([WildGuard](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.18495); [Northcutt et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.14749); [promptfoo blog](https://www.promptfoo.dev/blog/asr-not-portable-metric/)).
9. **Report an indeterminate rate and document every vendor mapping.** Count errors, timeouts and missing signals separately and never score them as allow. State which Bedrock filter strength and which signal, intervention or detection, counts as a block. Evidence: promptfoo's warning that missing metadata behaves like `flagged: false` and that detect-only findings are not interventions ([promptfoo](https://www.promptfoo.dev/docs/guides/testing-guardrails/)).
10. **Add a worst-category column.** Balanced accuracy averages away a weak job. Show each system's lowest job score next to the headline. Evidence: garak's minimum aggregation ([garak](https://reference.garak.ai/en/latest/reporting.html)).
11. **Make the cost and latency method explicit.** Publish list price and date, the token-length distribution used, region, and p50 and p95 latency with warm-up discarded, and mark hosted versus self-hosted timing as not comparable. Evidence: Artificial Analysis's protocol, and Fiddler being the only other source of per-check cost ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/guardrail-safety-benchmark); [Fiddler](https://www.fiddler.ai/guardrails-benchmarks/access)).
12. **State scope, dates and interests on the board.** Put a test date on every row, say the board measures direct classification on static rows and not end-to-end blocking or adaptive robustness, and state raxIT's own interest in any system it scores. Evidence: PINT's dated rows, promptfoo's direct-versus-integrated split, and every vendor comparison ranking its publisher first ([PINT](https://github.com/lakeraai/pint-benchmark); [promptfoo](https://www.promptfoo.dev/docs/guides/testing-guardrails/); [Fiddler](https://www.fiddler.ai/guardrails-benchmarks/access)).
13. **Keep what already beats the field.** Keep the fixed threshold, label-based scoring with no LLM judge, paired bootstrap with group resampling, Holm tiers, cost per 1,000 checks, Bedrock as the reference product, and a separate contract and board for edition 3. No surveyed guardrail benchmark does all of these.
