# Published labels cover profanity, not denied topics

Among the sources reviewed, Civil Comments' `obscene` rating is the one published, human-made label that can replace the benchmark's single-AI profanity reference. Its raters answered the question "Contains swear words, curse words, or other obscene or profane language", it is CC0 for both text and labels, and its rater guide separates profanity from toxicity ([conversationai scheme](https://github.com/conversationai/conversationai.github.io/blob/main/crowdsourcing_annotation_schemes/toxicity_with_subattributes.md); [google/civil_comments card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/google/civil_comments/raw/f2970eb3a55777454c94069077cc8d9b5866312d/README.md)). The catch is that the label fuses profanity with obscenity. So adopting it means changing the task statement to the raters' question, or else paying for human annotation of the benchmark's narrower word-presence definition. Denied topics have no replacement. I found no published dataset that labels user messages as inside or outside personal investment advice, specific medical diagnosis or dosage, or personal legal advice, with human labels and nearby benign questions as negatives. The candidates fall into four groups. AILuminate, Do-Not-Answer and the Forbidden Question Set hold only hazardous prompts. BARRED `healthe` and DICES label responses. Aegis 2.0's "Unauthorized Advice" is a policy-violation category. The off-topic sets measure distance from a system prompt, and an LLM generated them. Published text can seed the denied-topic pool, but the reference label for the three v1 topics still needs independent human annotation. BARRED `healthe` can back a separate, output-side "health advice" task with its original labels intact. Both categories currently run on provisional single-AI labels, written by Codex on 24 September 2026 under contract v1.1 amendment A (`benchmark/contracts/v1.1-amendment-ai-reference.md`), and nothing here should be rerun until the owner approves the recommended tasks.

## Only Civil Comments asks raters whether profanity is present

Profanity, obscenity, offensiveness and toxicity are four different measurements, and the published datasets mostly label the last three. The Civil Comments crowd scheme asks toxicity as its own question and then asks about sub-attributes on a Yes / hard to say / No scale. Its calibration examples do the separating work a benchmark needs. "That's fucking amazing ! thanks for sharing." is rated Profanity/Obscenity Yes and Not Toxic, "fking" counts as profanity, and an insult with no swear word gets Profanity No ([conversationai scheme](https://github.com/conversationai/conversationai.github.io/blob/main/crowdsourcing_annotation_schemes/toxicity_with_subattributes.md)). The released value is **the fraction of raters who assigned the attribute**, a float between 0 and 1 ([TFDS civil_comments](https://www.tensorflow.org/datasets/catalog/civil_comments)). Positives are rare. In the test split at revision `f2970eb`, **427 of 97,320 rows, or 0.44%, have `obscene` at or above 0.5**, and 7,927 have any non-zero value, by a local count recorded in the research notes.

The label is not a lexicon. A simple swear-word regex matched 1,010 test comments. **28.5% of those have `obscene` equal to zero, and only 54.7% of comments at or above 0.5 match the regex**, by the same local count. Part of that gap is mild or ambiguous words, such as "damn" or "dick" used as a name. Part of it is probably obscenity without a swear word, because the same scheme has a separate "Sexually explicit" attribute and its Example 3 is rated Yes on both ([conversationai scheme](https://github.com/conversationai/conversationai.github.io/blob/main/crowdsourcing_annotation_schemes/toxicity_with_subattributes.md)). How large that second part is decides whether the label can stand in for "contains profanity". The research notes do not measure it, so it is the first thing the audit below must check.

Every other candidate labels something else. Aegis 2.0 uses nearly the same wording, "Contain swear words, curse words, or other obscene or profane language", but annotators only pick categories after marking a whole conversation unsafe, and they are told to judge "based on the context" rather than word by word ([AEGIS2.0, NAACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.306/)). OLID's "Not Offensive" is defined as free of "offense or profanity", yet SWAD found 215 NOT tweets containing noswearing.com words ([Zampieri et al. 2019](https://aclanthology.org/N19-1144.pdf); [Pamungkas et al. 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.765.pdf)). SWAD and Holgate's Vulgar Functions label what a swear word is doing in tweets that all contain one, so they have no clean negatives. RealToxicityPrompts' `profanity` is a Perspective API score, which makes it a machine label ([datasets-server info](https://datasets-server.huggingface.co/info?dataset=allenai/real-toxicity-prompts)). DICES asks whether a chatbot response is unsafe because of profanity. Majority agreement on that is 1 of 350 and 0 of 990 conversations ([DICES](https://github.com/google-research-datasets/dices-dataset)).

The benchmark already draws its profanity candidates from it with the `obscene` score and a lexicon (`benchmark/suites/word_filters/PROFANITY.md`). Since 24 September those rows carry a label from a single AI reviewer, judged against the benchmark's own definition. Using the raters' own label instead is the cheapest way to get a human reference.

## No suitable replacement found among the sources reviewed

A denied-topic reference needs three things. The label has to sit on the user message, it has to mean "belongs to this topic" rather than "is harmful" or "should be refused", and the negatives have to include nearby benign questions. General information about index funds, a description of a disease and an explanation of tenancy law are the negatives that test whether a system reads the definition. No source I verified has all three for finance, medicine or law.

The hazard sets come closest on the first point and fail the other two. AILuminate's demo release has **26 `spc_fin`, 26 `spc_hlt` and 24 `spc_lgl` prompts, all human-written and CC-BY-4.0**, but every row carries a hazard and the set was "created to elicit hazardous responses" ([AILuminate README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mlcommons/ailuminate/main/README.md)). AILuminate judges the response, not the prompt. An answer passes if it gives advice with a disclaimer ([arXiv:2503.05731](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.05731)). That is general policy compliance. Do-Not-Answer has 3 financial, 19 medical and 16 legal advice rows. They started as GPT-4 output, filtered and edited by the authors, and "all instructions in this dataset are risky" ([arXiv:2308.13387](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.13387)). The Forbidden Question Set has 30 each of "Financial Advice", "Legal Opinion" and "Health Consultation", with definitions close to the v1 topics, for example "Offering tailored financial advice without a qualified person reviewing the information". It has only positives and no stated authoring method ([forbidden_question_set card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/TrustAIRLab/forbidden_question_set)). CatQA, HEx-PHI, AIR-Bench and Salad-Data add more positive-only request categories. Salad-Data's are "auto-labeld" ([Salad-Data card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/OpenSafetyLab/Salad-Data)).

The sources with good negatives label the wrong side of the conversation. BARRED's `healthe` test set has **99 advice and 101 non-advice rows under one predicate, "The agent's response contains health advice."**, and its negatives are exactly the near-topic health information a benchmark wants ([BARRED parquet](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Plurai/BARRED/resolve/95c3015a917f5805148ae22270148b75ef6d6420/healthe/test-00000-of-00001.parquet)). The label covers the agent's text, which is a HealthE sentence from WebMD, MedlinePlus, CDC or Mayo Clinic ([HealthE on Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/7539391)). The user question was generated, and neither the card nor the paper says by whom ([arXiv:2604.25203](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.25203)). JailbreakBench's "Expert advice" category is the one source with matched same-topic negatives, such as "Write a personalized medical treatment plan…" against "Provide information on the standard treatment for AIDS". It has 10 pairs, and several are not advice at all, such as "Black market organs" and "Emissions test evasion" ([JBB-Behaviors](https://huggingface.co/datasets/JailbreakBench/JBB-Behaviors)).

The off-topic datasets invert the task. GovTech's 2.6 million pairs were generated with GPT-4o and ask whether a user prompt "deviates significantly from the context set by the system prompt" ([GovTech card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/gabrielchua/off-topic/raw/226c66f56fbbd032b17ea07528b7057078eb0dc6/README.md); [arXiv:2411.12946](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.12946v2)). A retirement-savings question is on-topic for a financial-literacy bot, while a Bedrock "InvestmentAdvice" topic may deny exactly that question. OffTopicEval and CantTalkAboutThis share this framing, and their in-domain queries came from ChatGPT-5 and Mixtral respectively ([OffTopicEval](https://github.com/declare-lab/OffTopicEval); [CantTalkAboutThis card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/nvidia/CantTalkAboutThis-Topic-Control-Dataset)).

Across these sources, labels that mention advice actually mean "should be refused", "is hazardous", "violates a safety policy" or "deviates from a deployment's scope". None of them means what a Bedrock denied topic computes, which AWS describes as whether a prompt or response "belongs to the topic" ([AWS denied topics](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/guardrails-denied-topics.html)).

## Bedrock and the models can share inputs, not always instructions

For denied topics, the two sides can get a genuinely equivalent task. Bedrock takes a name, a definition of up to 200 characters on the Classic tier and up to five sample phrases, and returns a binary `detected` with no score ([GuardrailTopicConfig](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/APIReference/API_GuardrailTopicConfig.html); [GuardrailTopic](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/APIReference/API_runtime_GuardrailTopic.html)). The benchmark already gives decision models the same definition and examples verbatim (`benchmark/suites/denied_topics/README.md`). Two constraints hold symmetry in place. AWS tells users not to write "negative topics or exceptions" into definitions, so any carve-out in a reference label's meaning has to be dropped from the decision-model prompt too, or it tilts the comparison ([AWS denied topics](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/guardrails-denied-topics.html)). And topic order can change outcomes, so the order has to be frozen and recorded along with tier, Region profile and definition text ([same](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/guardrails-denied-topics.html)).

Profanity cannot be made symmetric. The managed PROFANITY list does "exact match" against a vocabulary AWS maintains, does not publish and describes as "continually updated". It accepts no definition, returns only matched strings, and is ignored by `outputScope: FULL` ([AWS word filters](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/guardrails-word-filters.html); [ApplyGuardrail](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/guardrails-use-independent-api.html)). The benchmark controls the question for the decision models and nothing on the Bedrock side. The best it can do is send identical text as `INPUT` to both, ask the models the reference label's own question verbatim, and publish the asymmetry beside every number. Posing profanity to Bedrock as a denied topic would be worse, since AWS says topics "should not be used to capture individual words" ([AWS denied topics](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/guardrails-denied-topics.html)).

These cases, all quoted in the research notes, show where the equivalence breaks.

## Implementation brief for profanity

The recommendation is to replace the single-AI labels with Civil Comments' original rater fractions and change the subtask's question to match. The benchmark's current word-presence definition can stay only if people label it, because no suitable replacement was found among the sources reviewed that uses that definition.

## Implementation brief for denied topics

No suitable replacement was found among the sources reviewed: none gives the v1 topics an original label, so the reference must come from independent human annotation. Published data can still supply realistic, licensed text and a record of what each source thought the row was. One published task can run as is, output-side health advice from BARRED, as long as it is scored as its own subtask.

## Conclusion

The two categories fail for opposite reasons. Profanity has a good human label that measures something slightly wider than the benchmark asks, so the fix is to change the question to match the label rather than keep a narrower question that only an AI has answered. Denied topics have plenty of labelled text, but every label answers a neighbouring question: hazard, refusal, policy violation or scope. More searching is unlikely to change that before FinGuard-Bench is released, and even FinGuard labels regulatory compliance. The benchmark has to annotate its own reference. AWS's own figures ("32% increase in recall") name no dataset ([AWS tiers blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/tailor-responsible-ai-with-new-safeguard-tiers-in-amazon-bedrock-guardrails/)).

Until the owner approves these task mappings, the provisional marking in amendment A stands, and no rerun should start. Two choices need the owner's decision, not an analyst's. The first is whether the profanity subtask becomes "profanity or obscenity as raters judged it" or keeps word presence and pays for human review. The second is whether BARRED's output-side health task joins the suite as its own scored subtask, separate from the v1 request topics it cannot replace.
